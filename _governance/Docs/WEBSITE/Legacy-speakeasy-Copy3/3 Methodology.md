METHODOLOGY PAGE — SPEAKEASY + RIGOR (ALIGNED)

Business Archaeology isn’t a metaphor.
It’s a method.
Not something abstract or interpretive—
 but a structured way of going back through a business and figuring out what actually happened, what mattered, and what never should have been left behind.
Most approaches start from the present and try to build forward.
This does the opposite.
It starts by looking at everything that already exists—
 not just what’s visible, but what’s been buried, scattered, or quietly abandoned over time.

Every engagement begins the same way.
Not with assumptions.
With a survey.
Where the business has existed.
 What’s been created.
 What’s still accessible—and what isn’t.
That initial pass is light by design.
Just enough to understand whether there’s something worth excavating,
 and where it might be located.
If there is, the process goes deeper.

Excavation isn’t random.
It’s systematic.
Across platforms, archives, internal records, and external signals,
 assets are recovered and placed back into sequence.
Not just what was created—
 but when, why, and what happened next.
Because isolated artifacts don’t mean much on their own.
Context is what gives them value.

That’s where most things have been lost.
Not because they didn’t work.
But because no one ever saw the full pattern.

To avoid that, everything is verified.
Nothing is taken at face value.
Each meaningful artifact is cross-checked against multiple independent sources—
 a process we refer to internally as the Three-Source Rule.
If something can’t be validated, it doesn’t get used.
If it can, it becomes part of a larger structure that starts to take shape as the excavation progresses.

Over time, what looked like disconnected pieces
 begins to organize itself into something coherent.
A timeline forms.
Decisions become visible.
Moments of traction—and moments of abandonment—stand out clearly.
And more importantly, the relationship between them becomes obvious.

That’s the turning point.
Not when something new is introduced.
But when what already happened finally makes sense.

From there, the work shifts.
Less about discovery.
More about restoration.
What’s still intact gets adapted.
What was incomplete gets rebuilt.
What was misaligned gets repositioned.
Not as isolated fixes—
 but as part of a structure that can actually hold together going forward.

This is where most people expect the process to end.
It doesn’t.
Because without preservation, everything eventually drifts again.

So the final stages are quieter.
Ongoing.
Making sure what’s been recovered continues to connect,
 continues to compound,
 and doesn’t fall back into the same patterns that buried it in the first place.

If you step back, the progression is consistent every time.
It starts with a survey.
Moves into excavation.
Transitions into restoration.
Then shifts into preservation.
And eventually, what’s been rebuilt becomes visible again—
 not as noise, but as signal.

That sequence isn’t optional.
Most of the problems this process solves
 come from skipping parts of it.

What this page describes isn’t a list of services.
It’s the structure behind everything that follows.
The same process that begins with a light survey
 can extend all the way through full excavation, restoration, and ongoing preservation—
 depending on how much there is to uncover, and how far you choose to take it.

Because in most cases, the question isn’t whether something valuable is there.
It’s whether anyone takes the time to go back and find it properly.

And that’s the difference.
Between starting over…
and uncovering what was already working
 before it was forgotten.
