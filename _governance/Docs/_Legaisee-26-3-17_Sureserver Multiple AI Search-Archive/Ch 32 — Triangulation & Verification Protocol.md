CHAPTER 32: The Triangulation & Verification Protocol
Introduction: The Problem of Conflicting Truth
Every source lies. Not intentionally—sources are biased by memory, perspective, self-interest, and the passage of time. The previous owner remembers things differently than the founder. The employee saw only their department. The customer experienced only the outcome, not the process.
Your job is not to find "the truth." Your job is to find the most probable version of events given available evidence, clearly labeled with confidence levels.
This chapter gives you the frameworks to:
Reconcile conflicting sources
Verify authenticity of discovered materials
Grade uncertainty appropriately
Present findings with intellectual honesty

THE SOURCE QUALITY HIERARCHY
Not all sources are equal. Establish this hierarchy in your analysis:
Tier 1: Contemporaneous Documentation (Highest Confidence)
What: Documents created at the time of events, with metadata, by disinterested parties or systems.
Examples:
Financial records with timestamps (invoices, budgets, bank statements)
Dated email archives with headers and thread history
Website captures with specific dates (Wayback Machine with timestamps)
Automated system logs (CRM timestamps, analytics data)
Third-party verification (industry publications, news coverage, awards databases)
Legal filings (incorporation documents, trademarks, patents with dates)
Why Highest: Created for operational purposes, not retrospective justification. Hard to fake convincingly at scale.
Confidence Label: [VERIFIED]

Tier 2: Participant Documentation (High Confidence)
What: Materials created by participants during or shortly after the era, for business purposes.
Examples:
Marketing materials with production dates
Internal memos and meeting minutes
Sales presentations and proposals
Design files with creation dates
Photographs with EXIF data or contextual dating
Audio/video recordings from the period
Why High: Created for immediate business use, not historical narrative. May have promotional bias but dates and facts are usually accurate.
Confidence Label: [PROBABLE]

Tier 3: Participant Memory (Medium Confidence)
What: Recollections of people who were there, interviewed after the fact.
Examples:
Founder interviews about origin story
Former employee descriptions of campaigns
Previous owner explanation of strategic decisions
Customer recollections of their experience
Why Medium: Memory degrades, reconstructs, and self-serves. Specific details (dates, numbers) often wrong; broad patterns often right.
Confidence Label: [PLAUSIBLE] (if corroborated) or [UNCERTAIN] (if isolated)

Tier 4: Hearsay and Inference (Lowest Confidence)
What: Secondhand information, speculation, or interpretation without direct evidence.
Examples:
"I heard that..." statements
"Someone told me..." claims
Industry gossip repeated as fact
Assumptions based on patterns ("they probably...")
Agenda-driven testimony (obvious bias, no corroboration)
Why Lowest: Multiple layers of potential distortion. Use only as leads to find better sources, never as primary evidence.
Confidence Label: [UNVERIFIED] or exclude entirely

THE 3-SOURCE RULE
For any significant claim in your audit report, require:
Minimum: 2 independent sources
Ideally: 3+ converging sources
Exception: 1 Tier 1 source alone is sufficient for [VERIFIED]
"Significant Claim" Defined:
Any statement about revenue, budget, or financial results
Any claim about dates, duration, or sequence of events
Any attribution of strategy or motivation to specific individuals
Any statement about "what worked" or "what failed"
"Independent" Defined:
Sources did not coordinate their stories
Sources have different perspectives (e.g., owner + employee + customer)
Sources have different incentives (not all self-serving in same direction)

HANDLING DIRECT CONTRADICTIONS
The Contradiction Taxonomy
When sources disagree, classify the contradiction:
Type A: Factual Contradiction (Dates, Numbers, Events)
Owner: "We launched that in 2005"
Employee: "No, it was definitely 2003"
Resolution: Find Tier 1 source (dated document, email, news coverage)
Type B: Interpretive Contradiction (Why Something Happened)
Owner: "We stopped because it wasn't working"
Employee: "We stopped because the owner got bored"
Resolution: Both can be partially true; document both perspectives with attribution
Type C: Scope Contradiction (Extent or Impact)
Owner: "That was our biggest campaign ever"
Employee: "It was one of many, nothing special"
Resolution: Find budget data, sales data, or third-party coverage to verify scale
Type D: Existence Contradiction (Whether Something Happened at All)
Owner: "We never did direct mail"
Employee: "I spent three years managing direct mail"
Resolution: Find physical artifacts (samples, invoices, photos) or conclude [UNCERTAIN]

AUTHENTICITY VERIFICATION
For Discovered Documents and Artifacts
Step 1: Provenance (Chain of Custody)
Document where this came from:
Who had it before you?
How did they get it?
When did they get it?
Has it been stored securely?
Any gaps in the chain?
Red Flag: "I found this in a dumpster" (possible, but verify carefully)
Green Flag: "This came from [specific person's] files, passed to [next person] when they retired"
Step 2: Internal Consistency
Check if the document makes sense:
Dates: Are they chronologically possible? (No marketing campaigns dated before company founding)
Names: Do the people mentioned exist in company records?
Technology: Does it reference technology that existed then? (No "email campaigns" in 1985)
Formatting: Is the design/software appropriate for the era? (No 2020 fonts in a "1995" document)
Step 3: External Corroboration
Verify claims against independent sources:
Does the campaign mentioned appear in news coverage?
Do the budget figures match financial summaries?
Do the dates align with known company events (moves, launches, leadership changes)?
Do competitors or industry publications mention this?
Step 4: Motive Analysis
Consider who benefits if this is believed:
Does it make one person look uniquely good?
Does it absolve someone of blame?
Does it align suspiciously perfectly with current narrative?
Is there evidence of selective editing (pages missing, sections crossed out)?
Red Flags for Forgery/Alteration:
Perfect preservation of "old" materials (too convenient)
Self-serving narrative without nuance or failure
Missing metadata (no dates, no author, no distribution list)
Refusal to allow independent verification ("Just trust me")
Anachronistic language or references

THE UNCERTAINTY GRADING SYSTEM
Every finding in your report gets a confidence label. Be explicit.
[VERIFIED]
Definition: Multiple Tier 1 sources confirm; no credible contradiction.
Use for: Dates of incorporation, award wins, patent filings, major financial transactions, existence of specific campaigns with budget documentation.
Example:
"[Company] won the Addy Award for Best Print Campaign in 1997. VERIFIED by award database entry, newspaper coverage in [Publication] dated [Date], and acceptance speech transcript in company files."

[PROBABLE]
Definition: Tier 1 source plus Tier 2 support, or multiple Tier 2 sources with minor gaps.
Use for: Specific budget figures (approximate), campaign timing, strategic motivations (if documented in memos), market positioning claims.
Example:
"[Company] likely invested $40,000-$60,000 annually in radio advertising from 1998-2000. PROBABLE based on budget fragment showing $48,000 (Tier 1) and employee confirmation of 'heavy radio presence' (Tier 2). Exact programming mix not documented."

[PLAUSIBLE]
Definition: Tier 2 or Tier 3 sources only, no direct contradiction, but insufficient for higher confidence.
Use for: Tactical details, specific creative approaches, customer reactions, competitive responses.
Example:
"[Company] may have tested direct mail in 2001-2002. PLAUSIBLE based on employee recollection of 'some mailings' (Tier 3) but no documents or financial records found. Could not confirm scale or results."

[UNCERTAIN]
Definition: Single source, or conflicting sources with no resolution, or significant evidence gaps.
Use for: Disputed events, poorly documented eras, claims that matter but cannot be verified.
Example:
"Scope of [Company]'s 2002 product launch is disputed. Marketing director claims 'our biggest campaign ever' (Tier 3); operations manager recalls 'routine launch, nothing special' (Tier 3). No budget documents or third-party coverage found to resolve. UNCERTAIN."

[UNVERIFIED]
Definition: Claim made, no supporting evidence found, possibly contradicted by available evidence.
Use for: Grandiose claims without basis, suspected falsehoods, family legend vs. documented reality.
Example:
"Founder claims 'we invented the [process] and dominated the market for a decade' (Tier 3). Patent search negative; industry publications show [competitor] introduced similar process 5 years earlier; market share data unavailable. UNVERIFIED."

THE REPORTING STANDARD
Always Report Uncertainty
Good:
"[Company] appears to have run significant print advertising from 1995-1999, based on employee testimony and sample ads recovered from private archives. PROBABLE but exact budget UNCERTAIN."
Bad:
"[Company] ran a $100,000 print campaign from 1995-1999." (Unsourced, specific, overconfident)
Good:
"Multiple sources confirm TV advertising presence 2000-2002, though previous owner disputes extent. Conservative estimate based on available evidence: $30,000-$50,000 annually. PROBABLE for existence; PLAUSIBLE for budget range."
Bad:
"[Company] dominated TV advertising in their market from 2000-2002." (Overstated, unsupported)
The Uncertainty Footnote
For every [UNCERTAIN] or [UNVERIFIED] finding, add:
"This gap does not weaken the overall analysis. The absence of evidence for [specific claim] is itself evidence of [poor documentation practices / era of transition / etc.], which informs our understanding of [Company]'s marketing evolution."

THE CLIENT CONVERSATION ABOUT UNCERTAINTY
When presenting findings with gaps, frame it as sophistication, not failure:
The Script
"I found strong evidence for [verified finding with multiple sources].
For [uncertain period], I have conflicting accounts. Here's what [Source A] says, here's what [Source B] says, and here's why they might both be partially right—[plausible resolution].
The gap doesn't weaken the overall analysis. It actually highlights where documentation broke down, which is part of the story. The fact that [Company] doesn't have clear records from [era] tells us something about how they managed knowledge during that transition."
Why This Works
Shows intellectual honesty (not making things up)
Demonstrates analytical depth (considering multiple perspectives)
Transforms weakness into insight (poor documentation = finding)
Sets up the value of your system (this is why we build documentation systems)

THE RECONCILIATION WORKSHOP
When the client has internal disagreement about their own history, offer to facilitate:
The Format: 90-Minute Facilitated Session
Participants:
Current owner
Previous owner (if willing and relationship permits)
Longtime employees (2-3, different eras if possible)
You (neutral facilitator, not advocate)
Your Role:
Not to determine "truth"
To document all perspectives
To identify convergences and divergences
To synthesize what evidence supports
The Agenda:
Minutes 0-10: Ground Rules
"This is not about right or wrong. It's about documenting what we know, what we remember, and where we disagree."
"All perspectives are valid. We're building a complete picture, not assigning blame."
"I will document everything. You'll review before anything is published."
Minutes 10-40: Individual Stories
Each person shares their version of [era/event] (10 minutes each)
No interruptions, no arguments
You take detailed notes
Minutes 40-55: Convergence Identification
"Where do your stories agree?" (List points of consensus)
"What does everyone confirm?" (Document shared facts)
Minutes 55-75: Divergence Exploration
"Where do you see it differently?"
For each disagreement: "How could both be true? Different time periods? Different departments? Different definitions?"
Explore: "What evidence would resolve this?"
Minutes 75-90: Synthesis and Documentation
You present: "Here's what the evidence supports..."
Group agrees on: Documented facts, disputed elements, confidence levels
Next steps: "I will draft the findings. Everyone reviews before finalization."
Outcome:
Unified (or at least documented) history
Reduced internal conflict
Richer report with multiple perspectives
Your role elevated to neutral arbiter

THE CONTINUOUS VERIFICATION PROTOCOL
New information emerges after report delivery. Handle it professionally.
Scenario A: Client Finds New Materials After Audit
Your Response:
"Excellent! This could fill gaps or correct errors. Let me review what you found."
Review Process:
Assess: Does this change any findings? Confirm? Contradict?
Determine: Minor addendum or major revision?
Delivery:
Minor (fills gap, confirms finding): Free addendum (1-2 pages)
Major (contradicts finding, requires rewrite): 50% of audit price for revised report
Never: Be defensive about errors. Be grateful for correction.

Scenario B: Previous Owner Disputes Findings After Publication
Your Response:
"Thank you for the specific feedback. Let me review the evidence for each point you raise."
Investigation:
For each disputed claim: Review your evidence
If you find error: Correct immediately, issue revised report, apologize specifically
If disagreement persists: Document alternative view in addendum
The Addendum Template:

ADDENDUM TO [COMPANY] BUSINESS HISTORY AUDIT
Date: [Date]
Revised Finding: [Specific claim being modified]
Original Finding: [What you reported]
New Information: [What emerged]
Analysis: [Why this changes or adds to original]
Alternative Perspective: [If source disputes and you maintain original, document their view]
Updated Confidence: [New confidence level]

Never:
Defend errors to save face
Attack the source of correction
Ignore legitimate corrections
Blame "methodology" for your mistake
Always:
Correct errors immediately and transparently
Thank the person who caught it
Update your process to prevent similar errors
View it as improving the product
