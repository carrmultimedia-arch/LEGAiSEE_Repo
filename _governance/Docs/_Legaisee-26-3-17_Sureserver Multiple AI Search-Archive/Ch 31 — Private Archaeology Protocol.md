CHAPTER 31: The Private Archaeology Protocol
Introduction: Beyond the Digital Footprint
Public archaeology finds what anyone can find. Private archaeology finds what no one else can find. This is where the real competitive advantage lies.
The best assets are often buried in:
Basements and storage units containing decades of physical artifacts
Retired hard drives with email archives and customer databases
Human memory of founders, former employees, and longtime customers
Private collections of the previous owner who built the business
This chapter gives you the frameworks, scripts, and negotiation tactics to access these private sources—and the protocols for handling what you find.

THE ACCESS NEGOTIATION FRAMEWORK
Getting access to private artifacts requires trust, tact, and persistence. This framework gives you the scripts and strategies for each scenario.
Scenario 1: The Current Owner Has the Goods
The Approach:
"I'm building a complete picture of your marketing history. The public record shows [X], but I know the real story is often in files, archives, and memories. Do you have any old marketing materials—files, photos, boxes of stuff—that might fill in the gaps?"
The Request Ladder (Start Small, Escalate):
Level 1: The Casual Mention
"I love finding old brochures and ads—do you have any lying around?"
Low pressure, easy to say yes or no
Often triggers: "Actually, there might be some boxes..."
Level 2: The Specific Request
"I'd love to see any direct mail you've kept, even if it's disorganized. I can sort through it."
Shows you're serious, willing to do the work
Addresses their objection: "It's a mess"
Level 3: The Storage Unit Visit
"Would it be worth a trip to your storage unit? I'll bring a scanner and we can digitize anything useful."
You bring value (scanner, organization)
Time-boxed (2 hours), specific outcome
Level 4: The Full Archive Access
"I'd like to spend a day going through your archives systematically. I'll organize as I go and leave everything better than I found it."
Full immersion, maximum yield
Requires high trust, offer clear value exchange

The Positive Relationship Approach:
"You speak highly of what [previous owner] built. I'm documenting the full history—including their era—so we don't lose those lessons. Would you be willing to introduce me? I'd love to hear their perspective and see any materials they saved."
The Neutral Relationship Approach:
"I'm a business historian working with [Company]. I'm documenting their marketing evolution from founding to present. [Previous owner]'s era is a critical part of that story. Would you be willing to make an introduction, or share their contact so I can reach out?"
The Negative Relationship Approach:
"I understand you and [previous owner] have history. I'm not taking sides—I'm documenting what [Company] did during their leadership. Their contributions were significant to where you are today. Would you object if I contacted them independently? Or if you prefer, I can work with other sources."
Key: Never force the issue. If current owner objects, respect it and work around with other sources.

Scenario 4: The Longtime Employee Interview
The Internal Champion Approach:
"You’ve been here [X] years. You’ve seen what worked, what failed, what changed. I’d love to buy you coffee and hear your perspective. Your institutional knowledge is incredibly valuable."
The Specific Story Extraction:
Don't ask: "Tell me about the old days." (Too broad, hard to answer)
Ask:
"Walk me through a typical [campaign/launch/sale] in [year]. What happened first, second, third?"
"What was the best [result/reaction] you remember? Tell me that story."
"What went wrong that taught you something important?"
"Who were the key people? What did each person do?"
"If you could do it again, what would you change?"
The "Day in the Life" Method:
"Pretend it's [year]. Walk me through your day from arrival to leaving. What were you working on? Who did you talk to? What were you trying to achieve?"
This triggers specific memories: "Oh, every morning we'd..."
Scenario 5: The Former Employee (No Longer There)
The LinkedIn Approach:

[Name],
I found your profile while researching [Company]'s history. You were there during [years]—a period I'm trying to understand better.
I'm documenting their marketing evolution (not investigating anything negative). I'd value your perspective on what worked then and how the market was different.
Worth a brief conversation?
— John Carr
The "Alumni" Framing:
"I'm researching [Company] alumni—people who built something there and moved on. Your experience is part of that history."
This frames them as valued contributor, not disgruntled ex-employee.

Step 2: Stabilize (Prevent Data Loss)

Never write to old drives. Always work from copies.
The Imaging Process:
Connect drive to write-blocking hardware or software

Work from the image, preserve original
Tools: dd (Linux), FTK Imager (Windows), Disk Utility (Mac)
The Cloud Account Protocol:
Export everything via official export tools (Google Takeout, etc.)
Don't delete or reorganize—preserve structure
Document original organization before any sorting

THE PHYSICAL ARTIFACT PROCESSING PROTOCOL
When you gain access to physical materials, process them systematically.
The On-Site Visit Kit
Bring:
Portable scanner (Fujitsu ScanSnap or similar)
Digital camera (for oversized items, 3D objects)
Laptop with large hard drive
USB drives for transfer
Gloves (for fragile/valuable items)
Notepad and pens
Water and snacks (long days)
The Scanning Protocol:
Organize by chronology (if possible) or by type
Scan front and back (even blank backs—may have notes)
Capture at 300+ DPI (archival quality)
File naming: [Date]_[Type]_[Description]_[Sequence].jpg
Example: 2005-03_Brochure_SpringSale_01of04.jpg
Create index spreadsheet as you scan
Backup to two locations before leaving site
The Photography Protocol (for 3D objects):
Booth displays: Wide shot, detail shots, context with person for scale
Promotional items: White background, top/side/bottom, detail of branding
Signage: Straight-on, angle, context/environment
Video/film: Note condition, format, prioritize digitization
The Post-Visit Processing
Within 48 hours of return:
Verify all scans (no corruption, readable)
Organize files into folder structure matching physical organization
Create finding aid (index with descriptions, dates, locations)
Return originals (if borrowed) or confirm storage location
Begin analysis (what's significant, what's missing)

THE HUMAN INTERVIEW PROTOCOL
The Pre-Interview Preparation
Research the Interviewee:
LinkedIn profile (career timeline, roles, connections)
Any public mentions (news articles, press releases)
Relationship to current/former owners
Potential biases or agendas
The Interview Guide Template:
Create a customized guide for each interviewee based on their role:
For Previous Owner/Founder:
Origin story: How did you start? First marketing?
Growth era: What tactics scaled the business?
Peak period: What was working best? Why?
Challenges: What almost killed the business?
Transition: Why did you sell/leave? What happened after?
Legacy: What are you most proud of? What was lost?
For Former Marketing Director:
Mandate: What were you hired to do?
Campaigns: What are you most proud of? Specifics (budget, results, creative)
Constraints: What limited your success?
Failures: What didn't work? Why?
Transition: Why did you leave? What changed after?
Advice: What would you do differently? What should they do now?
For Longtime Employee:
Timeline: When did you start? What role?
Evolution: How did marketing change over your tenure?
Tactics: What do you remember working? What failed?
Culture: What was the marketing philosophy?
People: Who were the key players? What did they do?
Current state: What do you think of where they are now?
The Interview Execution
Setting:
Their preference (their home, coffee shop, your office)
90 minutes scheduled (plan for 60, allow for 90)
Quiet, private, comfortable
Your recording setup tested (phone + backup)
The Opening (5 minutes):
"Thank you for doing this. I'm documenting [Company]'s marketing history from [year] to present. Your perspective is invaluable because [specific reason—'you were there during the growth,' 'you built the system,' etc.].
This is not an investigation—there's no right or wrong. I'm just trying to understand what happened, what worked, and why things changed.
May I record this? It helps me capture details accurately. You'll have approval over any quotes before I use them."
The Body (75 minutes):
Use the "funnel" approach:
Broad: "Tell me about [era]" (let them talk)
Specific: "You mentioned [specific thing]—tell me more about that"
Deeper: "Why do you think that worked?" "What was the reaction?"
Comparative: "How did that compare to [other tactic/era]?"
The Closing (10 minutes):
"This has been incredibly helpful. Two final questions:
What did I not ask that I should have?
Who else should I talk to?
And: May I follow up if I have clarifying questions? Can I send you a draft of my findings to review for accuracy?"
The Post-Interview Processing
Within 24 hours:
Transcribe (Otter.ai, Rev.com, or manual)
Highlight key quotes (mark for potential use)
Fact-check (verify dates, names, claims against other sources)
Send thank-you (email with specific reference to their contribution)
Update interview log (who, when, topics, follow-up needed)
The Quote Request (Before Publication):
"I'm preparing my findings for [Company]. You said [specific quote]. May I use this in the report? I can attribute it to you or use 'former [role]' if you prefer anonymity."

THE REFUSAL HANDLING PROTOCOL
When Access is Denied
The Graceful Acceptance:
"I completely understand. I respect your decision. If you ever change your mind, here's my card. And if you know anyone else who might be willing to talk about that era, I'd appreciate an introduction."
Never: Pressure, argue, or express frustration.
The Alternative Source Pivot:
If Previous Owner refuses:
Interview their former direct reports
Interview customers from their era
Search public records from that period (news, legal, industry pubs)
Look for "triangulation" sources (competitors, vendors, partners)
The Partial Access Maximization:
If they agree to interview but not document sharing:
Maximize the interview (2 hours, detailed)
Ask them to describe documents in detail ("What did the 2005 brochure say?")
Request they review your findings for accuracy ("Can I send you my draft to check?")
When Partial Information is Provided
The "Redaction" Offer:
"I understand some of this is sensitive. I can redact specific details—show you the final report and you tell me what to remove. Or I can summarize themes without quoting specific documents."
The "Embargo" Approach:
"Can we agree that I see everything, but nothing goes in the report without your specific approval? You have veto power over any detail."

THE PRIVACY & LEGAL BOUNDARIES
What You Can Access (With Permission)
✅ Marketing materials created for public distribution (even if never distributed)
✅ Internal strategy documents (with owner/company permission)
✅ Anonymized customer data (aggregated, no individual identification)
✅ Financial summaries (marketing budgets, ROI reports)
✅ Employee perspectives (with their consent)
✅ Process documentation (how they worked, not personnel files)
What You Cannot Access (Without Specific Permission)
❌ Individual customer data (names, addresses, purchase history, contact info)
❌ Employee personnel files (performance reviews, disciplinary records, salaries)
❌ Legal documents (settlements, disputes, litigation, attorney communications)
❌ Trade secrets (proprietary formulas, processes not related to marketing)
❌ Medical/health information (HIPAA-protected data)
❌ Financial account information (bank accounts, credit cards, detailed financials)
